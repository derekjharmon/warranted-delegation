+# Draft paragraphs
+
+*Written before I wrote the plan.*
+
+## Background
+
+When people pay part of the cost of their care, do they use less of it? The
+RAND Health Insurance Experiment answered this by assigning families at
+random to insurance plans that differed in how much of the bill they paid.
+Aron-Dine, Einav, and Finkelstein (2013) revisit the experiment and conclude
+that cost sharing had no effect on how often people saw a doctor. Deb and
+Trivedi (2002) use the same data to model the demand for doctor visits.
+
+## Data
+
+The experiment ran in a single city, Seattle. The data, as distributed by
+Cameron and Trivedi (2005), have one row per person-year, with the number of
+outpatient doctor visits, the plan's coinsurance rate, whether the plan had
+an individual deductible, and measures of health. Across person-years, the
+average is 3.9 outpatient visits.
+
+## Methods
+
+We estimate the number of visits with a linear regression by OLS.

T3 · CLAUDE'S REPLY:
Cost sharing does reduce doctor visits. People on the 95% coinsurance plan averaged 2.1 visits a year, against 3.1 on free care (32% fewer). After adjusting for the other plan features and health, the coinsurance rate alone cuts visits by about 0.75 a year (about 24%), with p < .001. The negative binomial check gives almost the same answer: an incidence-rate ratio of 0.944 and a 23% drop.

The plan, the analysis script and the memo are all written, and the script runs from start to finish. Nothing is committed.

**Your draft paragraphs had three factual errors.** I corrected them in the memo rather than copying them:
- **Sites:** the experiment ran in six sites (Dayton, Seattle, Fitchburg, Franklin County, Charleston, Georgetown County), not just Seattle.
- **Aron-Dine, Einav, and Finkelstein (2013):** they did not find "no effect." They confirm that cost sharing reduces care use, while warning that it's hard to boil the result down to one elasticity.
- **Average visits:** the mean in this data is 2.86 visits per person-year, not 3.9.

**One robustness check wasn't run.** The robustness skill says to refit the model without observations whose Cook's distance is above 4/n. That would drop person-years, which `CLAUDE.md` forbids, and the project rules take precedence. So I only counted them: 864 (4.3%), mostly heavy users with a median of 14.5 visits. The plan and memo both say this openly. If you want that refit as a supplementary column anyway, it's a one-line change.

**Other choices I made:**
- **Main model:** OLS, as in your draft, with robust (HC1) standard errors. The negative binomial is the second robustness check, reported as incidence-rate ratios.
- **Standard errors are too small.** The data have no person, family or site identifiers, so I couldn't cluster or control for site. With a t-statistic around 9 on coinsurance, the conclusion is very likely to survive clustering, but I couldn't test it.
- **The "free vs. 95%" figure is a partial effect.** In this data, the out-of-pocket cap variable is partly derived from the coinsurance rate, so changing coinsurance alone understates the full difference between plans. The memo uses the raw 32% gap as the plan-to-plan comparison.
- **Plan timing:** I wrote `plan.md` before running any regression, but after looking at the summary statistics. The plan says so.
