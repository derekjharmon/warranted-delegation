Cost sharing does reduce doctor visits in these data. Compared with free care, a 95% coinsurance plan is predicted to cut outpatient visits by about 21% (95% CI 16% to 26%), from about 3.3 to 2.6 visits a year. The individual deductible plan cuts them by about 22% (IRR 0.78, 95% CI 0.74 to 0.82). Each effect, and the joint test of both, is significant at p < .001, and neither robustness check changes the result.

The plan is in `plan.md`, the code in `analysis/run_analysis.py`, the tables in `output/`, and the memo in `memo.md` (about 1,500 words with tables, roughly three pages). The script runs and the memo's numbers match its output.

**Your draft paragraphs had errors, which I corrected in the memo:**
- **Aron-Dine, Einav and Finkelstein (2013)** did not conclude that cost sharing had no effect. They confirm that more cost sharing reduces use of care; their caution is about treating the result as one price elasticity (the well-known −0.2).
- **Sites:** the experiment ran at six sites (Dayton, Seattle, Fitchburg, Franklin County, Charleston, Georgetown County), not only Seattle.
- **Mean visits:** the average is 2.86, not 3.9.
- **Method:** I used a Poisson model instead of OLS, because visits are a skewed count with 31% zeros, and reported incidence-rate ratios as CLAUDE.md requires. OLS is in an appendix table for comparison and gives the same answer.

**The robustness skill clashed with your project rules.** The skill says to refit after dropping observations with Cook's distance above 4/n. That would remove person-years, and CLAUDE.md says to keep every one. I followed CLAUDE.md. Instead of dropping the 3,459 flagged person-years, I capped their visits at the 99th percentile (21), which changed 183 of them. The coinsurance IRR moved only from 0.949 to 0.953. This is stated in both the plan and the memo. If you'd rather run the skill's version exactly, it's a one-line change.

The second check, a negative binomial model, gives a coinsurance IRR of 0.944 and a 23% reduction.

**Limits of this dataset (covered in the memo):**
- **Standard errors are probably too small.** Plans were assigned by family and people appear in several years, but the data have no family or person ID, so errors can't be grouped by family. The effects are 7 to 9 standard errors from zero, so the conclusion should hold.
- **Missing controls:** there are no site or demographic variables.
- **The 21% is a partial effect.** It holds fixed the plan's cap on out-of-pocket spending, which is itself part of the plan design. That's why it is about half the raw 41% gap between free care and 95% coinsurance.

I rewrote the memo's interpretation so it covers only the rates the experiment tested: free care, 25%, 50% and 95% coinsurance. Before I go further, your reason for keeping the model doesn't fit the model the analysis actually uses.

**The model.** The main model is a **Poisson** regression. Poisson assumes the variance equals the mean, and it has no separate part for zeros. Here the variance is about seven times the mean, and 31% of person-years have no visits. I chose Poisson because its estimate of average visits stays valid even when that assumption fails, and the robust standard errors allow for it. But it is not a model built for this kind of data.
