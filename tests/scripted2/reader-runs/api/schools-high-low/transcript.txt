+# Draft paragraphs

+*Written before I settled the model.*

+## Background
+
+Achievement depends both on what students bring to school and on what
+schools spend on them. Hanushek (1997) reviewed decades of studies relating
+school resources to student achievement and found that smaller classes and
+higher spending raised achievement in most of the studies he reviewed.
+Krueger (1998), using the Tennessee STAR experiment, found that students
+randomly assigned to smaller classes scored higher on standardized tests.
+
+## Data
+
+The data are California's 1998 Standardized Testing and Reporting (STAR)
+results for ninth graders in mathematics, as compiled by Gill (2001). They
+have one row per county, covering all 303 of California's counties. On
+average, 47.3 percent of a county's ninth graders scored above the national
+median in math.
+
+## Methods
+
+We estimate a linear regression of the share of ninth graders above the
+national median by OLS.

## Main result

Student makeup predicts districts' math results much more than district resources do.

- **Low-income share** is the strongest predictor. Each additional percentage point of low-income students goes with about 0.46 points fewer students above the national median (p = 5.1e−28).
- **Racial and ethnic makeup** and the **share of students in UC/CSU prep courses** (+0.12 per point, p = .00094) also clearly predict results.
- **Resources show no detectable link.** Per-pupil spending, pupil–teacher ratio, teacher salary and teacher experience are all non-significant.
- **These results hold in both robustness checks.** The exception is a small negative link with year-round schools (p = .036), which disappears in the log-odds check.

## Approach

- **Model:** I used a fractional logit (Papke & Wooldridge, from your references) rather than the OLS in your draft. The outcome is a share between 0 and 1, and this model keeps predictions inside that range.
- **Reporting:** results are average marginal effects in percentage points, with HC1 standard errors, two-tailed tests and exact p-values. Percentage variables stay on the 0–100 scale.
- **Robustness:** the methods-kit skill specified two checks, and both are columns in the table. One drops districts with Cook's distance above 4/n; the other is OLS on the log-odds of the share.
- **Bug I fixed:** the first run of the influential-observations check dropped zero districts. statsmodels' default binomial setting fixes the dispersion at 1, while it is really about 0.03 for this outcome, so every Cook's distance came out too small. With the dispersion estimated properly, 18 districts are dropped; the estimates and standard errors don't change. I explain this in the memo.

## Corrections to your draft notes

I fixed these in the memo but left `notes/` unchanged:

1. **Hanushek (1997) says the opposite of what the draft says.** He found no strong or consistent relationship between spending or class size and achievement. The draft says he found positive effects in most studies.
2. **The Krueger article is 1999, not 1998.** Volume 114(2) of the QJE is 1999. I corrected the citation and the reference list.
3. **The rows are school districts, not counties.** California has only 58 counties. The statsmodels documentation says "counties," which is probably where that came from.
4. **The average share is 43.7%, not 47.3%.** Pooled across all students it's 40.5%, because large districts score lower.

One thing I couldn't check: the statsmodels documentation doesn't say which grade the scores are for. I kept your "ninth graders"; please confirm it against Gill (2001).
