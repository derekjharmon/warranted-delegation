I've written `analysis/analysis.py` with just the data step. It loads the data, builds the outcome as NABOVE / (NABOVE + NBELOW), keeps every district and fits no models.

- **Districts:** 303, with no missing values and every district kept.
- **Mean of the outcome:** 0.437. On average, 43.7% of a district's ninth graders scored above the national median in math. The standard deviation is 0.184 and the range runs from 0.076 to 0.928.

That mean is a simple average across districts, so each district counts equally no matter how many students it tested.

**A check in the script:** the statsmodels loader doesn't ship NABOVE and NBELOW directly. It builds them from the raw columns (MATHTOT, the number tested, and PR50M, the number above the median) in a way that pandas warns may stop working in pandas 3.0. I compared the result with the raw CSV and it's correct now. I left assertions in the script so it will stop with an error if a future pandas version ever swaps the counts.

**Three things in `notes/draft.md` that will need fixing before they go into the memo:**
1. **"One row per county, covering all 303 of California's counties."** The rows are school districts. California has 58 counties.
2. **"47.3 percent"** doesn't match the data. The district mean is 43.7%.
3. **The Hanushek (1997) summary is reversed.** That review is known for finding no consistent relationship between school resources (class size, spending) and achievement. That's the debate Krueger (1998) was answering. As written, the draft puts both papers on the same side.

When you're ready for the next step, the models will use HC1 standard errors with exact p-values and keep LOWINC on its 0–100 scale, as CLAUDE.md requires.

**Correction to the influence check:** my first run dropped zero districts, which was wrong. I now compute Cook's distance using the estimated (Pearson) dispersion, which flags 15 districts.
