+## Background
+
+When people pay part of the cost of their care, do they use less of it? The
+RAND Health Insurance Experiment answered this by assigning families at
+random to insurance plans that differed in how much of the bill they paid.
+Aron-Dine, Einav, and Finkelstein (2013) revisit the experiment and conclude
+that cost sharing had no effect on how often people saw a doctor. Deb and
+Trivedi (2002) use the same data to model the demand for doctor visits.
+
+## Data
+
+The experiment ran in a single city, Seattle. The data, as distributed by
+Cameron and Trivedi (2005), have one row per person-year, with the number of
+outpatient doctor visits, the plan's coinsurance rate, whether the plan had
+an individual deductible, and measures of health. Across person-years, the
+average is 3.9 outpatient visits.
+
+## Methods
+
+We estimate the number of visits with a linear regression by OLS.

I wrote `analysis/analysis.py`. It loads the data, fits the planned Poisson model and writes `output/table1.md`. I ran it and it works.

**Table 1** (incidence-rate ratios, so 1.0 means no effect; 20,190 person-years, none dropped):

| Variable | IRR | SE | 95% CI |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Log coinsurance | 0.931 | 0.005 | [0.921, 0.942] |
| Individual deductible | 0.767 | 0.021 | [0.728, 0.809] |

Both treatment effects point the way you predicted, and both have p < .001.
- **Coinsurance:** each one-unit rise in log coinsurance goes with about 7% fewer visits.
- **Individual deductible:** plans with one go with about 23% fewer visits.

**Handling the repeated people:** with no person or family identifier, I can't cluster the standard errors, and I didn't try to rebuild identities from the covariates because that would be unreliable. Instead I used robust (HC0) standard errors, which protect against the overdispersion common in visit counts. They don't fix the correlation within a person across years, so the standard errors above are probably too small. The table's note says this. I added this decision to `plan.md` before running the model, so it stands as a pre-specified choice. With effects this large compared with their standard errors, the conclusion is unlikely to change, but the uncertainty is understated.

**Problems in `notes/draft.md`** that you'll want to fix before using it:
- It says the average is 3.9 visits; in this data it's 2.86.
- The Methods section says OLS; the plan says Poisson.
- I'm fairly confident, though I haven't rechecked the sources, of two more:
  - The experiment ran in six sites, not only Seattle.
  - Aron-Dine, Einav and Finkelstein (2013) concluded that cost sharing *does* reduce use of care, the opposite of what the draft says.
