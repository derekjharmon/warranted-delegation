Blackburn worked for the contractor Rockwell and led a Materials Control and Specifications group of about 30 people.
- **Thomas, Singer, Pessin, Odom, Sheehan:** the transcripts confirm the roles in your table.

- **Who conducted them:** NASA historians Rebecca Wright and Jennifer Ross-Nazzal, as named in each title block and opening paragraph.

The eleven transcripts are now in `transcripts/`: ten PDFs plus Moser's 5/27/2010 session, each with a `.txt` version. The `.txt` files have one form-feed page break per PDF page, so the page numbers below match the PDF footers.

Six parallel readers each read one or two transcripts in full. I then checked about 35 of their quoted phrases against the text, and all but one were on the pages they reported. That one was Mitchell's "simplest elements" quote, which is on p. 30, not p. 31; it's corrected below. The "N of M pages" figures are the readers' estimates. **Royce Mitchell** (NASA Marshall; engineer, then manager; 46 pp.) – Almost entirely about Challenger. He was project manager for the redesigned solid rocket motor from 1986 to about 1990, chosen partly because he "was not on the project at the time of the accident" (p. 5). Columbia gets one remark on p. 30: "the two simplest elements … killed the two crews." About 15–18 pages concern settling technical disputes as a manager:
- p. 9: two competing redesign teams.
- pp. 17–18, 24: clashes with the National Research Council oversight panel over the deliberately flawed test motor.
- pp. 20–26: the outer boot ring change he "let [him]self get talked into," which failed in test and led to "a more painful day" before Truly (p. 26).
- pp. 36–37: formal design reviews.
- pp. 38–39: the debate over cork debris.
- pp. 30–31: a Thiokol worker secretly tamping down putty blowholes in test motors ("almost criminal"). This whole passage is in editorial brackets, so it was probably added when Mitchell reviewed the transcript, not spoken in the interview.

**John W. Thomas** (NASA engineer and manager until 1989, then Lockheed; 29 pp.) – Challenger only; Columbia never appears. He "essentially ran the Accident Analysis Panel" (pp. 2–5), then led the solid rocket motor redesign team (pp. 5–29). About 10–12 pages are the team leader settling technical questions, not raising concerns from below:
- p. 9: "we moved on as I had the final vote."
- pp. 10–12: tests to settle open hypotheses.
- pp. 8, 14–18: outside oversight, including heaters the panel insisted on that his team thought unnecessary.
- p. 20: Kennedy inspections he found "overzealous."

It reads as a tidy success story with little dissent.

**Gerald W. Smith** (NASA Marshall; engineer, then manager; 52 pp.) – Mainly Challenger. He was in Washington as NASA Headquarters' main engine expert when it happened (p. 26), then became Solid Rocket Booster project manager after Mulloy (pp. 27–43). On pp. 49–50 he says Marshall candidates "had been implicated by … the telecon the night before." Columbia gets one passage on debris "finger pointing" (p. 35). This is one of the richest transcripts, with about 15–17 pages of specific episodes:
- p. 2: as a young engineer, told no when he asked to change the tile.
- pp. 17–19: changing charts "in ways I didn't even like" after an internal review.
- pp. 22–23: pushed by Lindstrom past Engineering's official position to give his own opinion, which upset his boss.
- pp. 31–32: "I lost the argument" over the flawed-motor tests.
- pp. 32–34: after Challenger, an engineering staff that was "very risk-averse" and wrote 6,000–7,000 review discrepancies.
- pp. 37–38: friction with the new independent Quality office.
- p. 41: an all-night debate over a heater short before he said "go ahead and wake the crew."

**Jody A. Singer** (NASA Marshall; manager throughout, no engineering work described; 20 pp.) – Columbia is the focus. Her first launch as reusable solid rocket motor project manager was STS-107 (p. 3), and pp. 8–16 cover return to flight. Challenger is only career context (p. 1). About 9–10 pages concern concerns, but they are general and procedural rather than episodes:
- "Did we make it safe enough?" (pp. 8–10).
- Return to flight gave people leeway to ask "Why?" (pp. 13–14).
- "I believe you, but demonstrate it" (p. 15).
- Launch-readiness polling and the mission management team (pp. 17–19).

The bolt-catcher story (pp. 11–12) is secondhand. Her dates conflict: RSRM manager "2002 until 2005" (p. 3) vs. "2002 to 2007" (p. 8).

**Myron "Mike" Pessin** (Chrysler contractor briefly, then NASA Marshall; engineer, then External Tank chief engineer from 1988; 60 pp.) – Both accidents, but mostly tank design and manufacturing history. Challenger, pp. 44–47: the tank as "a victim … not the aggressor." Afterward, every repair record was reviewed and crack decisions were taken from floor quality supervisors and given to engineering. Columbia, pp. 27–29, is the key passage for your question:
- Foam loss "was always a refurbish issue for the orbiter. It was never flight safety."
- The tank project's deputy tried to open an in-flight anomaly two flights before the accident and was told "we know about foam coming off."
- The bipod fix was then handled as a routine change.

He had retired by then and declines to discuss the investigation (pp. 55–56). About 8–10 pages are directly relevant; smaller episodes are on pp. 18–23, 34–37, 45 and 49–52.

**James B. Odom** (NASA Marshall; External Tank project manager for 11 years, later director of engineering; 30 pp.) – Barely touches either accident. Columbia is never mentioned. On Challenger he says only that he was "enjoined" from working on it (p. 22). The ice and foam requirement on pp. 8–9 and 17 is not linked to an accident. Only about 2–3 pages concern concerns, and those are design and test lessons (pp. 16–17, 20, 25). It is the weakest fit for your question.
