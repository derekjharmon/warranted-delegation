Motivating dull work without paying more: a preregistered reanalysis of an 18-arm experiment

Abstract

Managers often try to raise effort without raising pay: they remind people that the work matters, compare them with coworkers, rank them, or give small gifts. We reanalyze DellaVigna and Pope's (2018) online experiment, in which about 10,000 workers did the same dull ten-minute task under one of 18 randomly assigned motivators, using an analysis plan written before we compared outcomes across arms. Three costless motivators raised effort over no piece rate: a request to try hard (the original study's "task significance" arm) by 213 points, ranking by 230 and social comparison by 336, against a baseline of 1,522. The request to try hard and ranking fell short of even a token piece rate of 1 cent per 1,000 points. Social comparison was not shown to differ from it. All three fell short of 1 cent per 100 points. An unconditional 40-cent gift had no detectable effect. A token piece rate did not crowd out effort: it raised it by 367 points. Higher piece rates raised effort further up to 4 cents per 100 points, but not detectably beyond. Our plan was not blind to the published results, and our estimates reproduce them.

1. The question

Pay is the motivator organizations reach for first. It is also the most expensive. The alternatives are cheap or free: telling people why the work matters or asking for their best effort, showing them how others perform, ranking them, or giving a bonus to build goodwill. For a manager, two things matter. Does the alternative raise effort at all? And how far does it get compared with a small amount of pay?

DellaVigna and Pope (2018) ran one of the few experiments that can answer both questions at once. Workers on Amazon Mechanical Turk did the same task, pressing two keys alternately for ten minutes. Each worker was randomly assigned one of 18 versions of a single instruction paragraph. Some versions varied pay, and others offered a psychological motivator or a gift with no pay at all. Because the arms differ only in that paragraph, any difference in effort between them is caused by the motivator.

We reanalyze their data for an organizational audience, around three hypotheses we fixed in advance:

  • H1, pay. (a) A higher piece rate raises effort more than a lower one. (b) Whether a small piece rate raises effort over paying nothing is an open question: a small payment can lower performance below no payment (Gneezy and Rustichini, 2000).
  • H2, psychological motivators. Task significance, social comparison and ranking raise effort over no piece rate, but less than the smallest piece rate.
  • H3, the gift. An unconditional gift raises effort, as gift exchange predicts.

2. The experiment and the data

Workers received a $1 flat fee for completing the task. They then had ten minutes to press "a" and "b" alternately, scoring a point for each a-b pair. The 18 arms were as follows:

  • Pay: no piece rate, and four piece rates paid within 24 hours (1 cent per 1,000 points, and 1, 4 and 10 cents per 100 points).
  • No extra pay: three psychological motivators (task significance, social comparison, ranking).
  • The gift: a 40-cent bonus paid regardless of score.
  • Nine other arms: charity pay, delayed pay, gain and loss framing of a threshold bonus, and two lotteries.

The three motivators and the gift are minimal versions of what organizations do. Their full paragraphs, as listed in the study's registration (AEARCTR-0000714), are:

  • Task significance: "Your score will not affect your payment in any way. We are interested in how fast people choose to press digits and we would like you to do your very best. So please try as hard as you can."
  • Social comparison: "Your score will not affect your payment in any way. In a previous version of this task, many participants were able to score more than 2,000 points."
  • Ranking: "Your score will not affect your payment in any way. After you play, we will show you how well you did relative to other participants who have previously done this task."
  • The gift: "In appreciation to you for performing this task, you will be paid a bonus of 40 cents. This bonus will be paid to your account within 24 hours. Your score will not affect your payment in any way."

What "task significance" tests here. We keep the original authors' labels, but the "task significance" arm does not tell workers that their work matters to anyone or benefits anyone. It says the researchers are interested in the result and asks workers to try as hard as they can. It is therefore a request for effort, not task significance in the sense of showing workers how their work helps others. We read its results that way.

The data. The outcome is points scored. Our file has 12,838 workers who started the task (output/tables/sample_flow.csv). Our notes describe it as the authors' file before their cleaning, from the replication package for the published article. We did not locate that package, so we cannot confirm the file's provenance directly. Two facts support it:

  • Its row count equals the 12,838 starters the original paper reports.
  • Applying the paper's exclusions reproduces its published arm means in all 18 arms to within 4 points (section 5.5).

The second check is also consistent with our codes for the three motivator arms, which pay nothing and so could not be checked from payouts.

3. What we planned, and what we had already seen

Before comparing any outcome across arms, we wrote an analysis plan (preregistration.md). It fixes the sample rules, the 15 confirmatory comparisons, the model, the multiple-comparison correction, the sensitivity samples, and what result would count against each hypothesis. We follow it exactly and label anything outside it.

The plan was not blind to the published results. While checking sources, before writing it, we saw two of the paper's arm means: 1,883 points for 1 cent per 1,000, and 1,521 for no piece rate. We also saw the abstract's summary that psychological motivators work but less well than incentives, and that experts wrongly expected crowd-out. The first bears on H1b and the second on H2. Readers should treat this paper as a preregistered reanalysis that confirms the published results, not an independent test of them.

The model. Points are regressed on 17 arm indicators, with no piece rate as the reference group. There are no covariates, and the standard errors are heteroskedasticity-robust (HC2). Every comparison is a difference in mean points between two arms, with a 95% confidence interval.

Multiple comparisons. The 12 primary comparisons are corrected together with Holm's (1979) method, at a family-wise error rate of 0.05. The three secondary comparisons are corrected as a separate family.

Language. "Significant" means a Holm-adjusted p below 0.05. A non-significant result means "not shown," never "no effect."

One correction to the plan: it describes nine exploratory arms as "the other seven arms," but lists all nine, and we analyze all nine.

4. The sample

The main sample applies the rules registered before the data were collected (output/tables/sample_flow.csv):

No score recorded 466 10,154

Dropping missing scores is not a registered rule. It is forced, since those workers have no outcome. Each arm has 542 to 580 workers (output/tables/sample_flow_by_arm.csv).

We also report four sensitivity samples, each the main sample minus a group of workers (output/tables/sample_flow.csv):

  • S1 drops 146 workers whose work was not approved, a rule the original authors added after registering (9,965 left).
  • S2 drops 137 workers who started during a stretch when the survey software's score counter malfunctioned (9,974 left). The original paper reports the malfunction but not its dates. We located the window from start times and missing scores alone, pooled across arms.
  • S3 drops 2 workers who finished in under ten minutes (10,109 left).
  • S1+S2 combines the first two and is closest to the original paper's sample (9,831 left).

Attrition. Among assigned workers, the share retained in each arm ranges from 0.820 to 0.870. Retention does not differ detectably across arms (χ² = 21.38, df = 17, p = 0.21), so we found no sign that exclusions drive the results. Under the plan, this meant the Lee (2009) bounds were not needed (output/tables/attrition.csv, output/tables/attrition_test.csv).

5. Results

The figure shows mean effort in every arm, with 95% intervals (output/figures/arm_means.png). The underlying numbers are in output/tables/arm_means.csv. Both are descriptive and not in the plan. The confirmatory comparisons are in output/tables/confirmatory_main.csv, and the plan's verdicts are in output/tables/hypothesis_verdicts.csv.

5.1 Pay (H1)

A token piece rate does not crowd out effort (H1b). The plan predicted no direction here. Paying 1 cent per 1,000 points raised effort over no piece rate by 367 points (95% CI 285 to 449, Holm p < 0.001). Paying 1 cent per 100 raised it by 507 points (426 to 588, Holm p < 0.001). In this setting, Gneezy and Rustichini's (2000) finding does not replicate: a payment too small to matter financially still raised effort by about a quarter over no pay. The average bonus actually paid in that arm was 1.5 cents (output/tables/exploratory_cost.csv, exploratory).

Higher pay raises effort, but only up to a point (H1a: two of three steps supported).

Step Difference (points) 95% CI Holm p Verdict
1 cent per 100 over 1 cent per 1,000 +140 63 to 217 0.002 as predicted
4 cents over 1 cent per 100 +101 27 to 175 0.030 as predicted
10 cents over 4 cents per 100 +43 −27 to 113 0.448 not significant

The third step is unsupported, not refuted. Its interval cannot rule out a modest gain, but the gain from 4 to 10 cents, if any, is small next to the steps below it. Effort is most responsive to the first, smallest amounts of pay.

5.2 Psychological motivators (H2)

The plan treats H2 as a joint claim for each motivator. It counts as supported only if the motivator raises effort over no piece rate and falls short of the smallest piece rate (1 cent per 1,000).

Motivator Over no piece rate Versus 1 cent per 1,000 Versus 1 cent per 100 (secondary) Verdict
Task significance +213 (131 to 296), p < 0.001 −153 (−232 to −75), p = 0.001 −293, p < 0.001 supported
Ranking +230 (145 to 315), p < 0.001 −137 (−218 to −56), p = 0.005 −277, p < 0.001 supported
Social comparison +336 (249 to 423), p < 0.001 −31 (−114 to 52), p = 0.464 −171, p < 0.001 partly supported

Differences in points (95% CI); p-values are Holm-adjusted.

All three motivators raised effort over no piece rate, and all three fell short of 1 cent per 100 points. Task significance and ranking also fell short of 1 cent per 1,000. Social comparison is only partly supported. It raised effort over no pay but was not shown to fall short of the smallest piece rate. Its interval runs from 114 points below that arm to 52 above, so we cannot say whether it falls short.

5.3 The gift (H3)

The unconditional 40-cent gift raised effort by 64 points over no piece rate. The interval runs from −20 to 149, and the Holm-adjusted p is 0.40. H3 is not supported. The plan counts against H3 only a significantly negative difference, so the data neither support gift exchange here nor refute it. In practical terms, a gift that cost 40 cents a worker bought no detectable effort. A piece rate that paid 1.5 cents on average bought 367 points (output/tables/exploratory_cost.csv).

5.4 How far the costless motivators get

Over no pay, the three motivators raised effort by 14% to 22%, against 24% for the token piece rate and 33% for 1 cent per 100 points. As a share of the gain from 1 cent per 100, ranking and the request to try hard got a little under half (0.45 and 0.42), social comparison got about two-thirds (0.66, close to the token piece rate's 0.72), and the gift got 0.13 (output/tables/exploratory_gap_share.csv; exploratory point estimates without intervals, with the tests above governing).

5.5 Robustness

Sensitivity samples. No verdict changes in any sensitivity sample: no estimate changes sign, and none crosses the significance threshold (output/tables/sensitivity_summary.csv, output/tables/confirmatory_sensitivity.csv). The gift estimate grows from 64 points in the main sample to 72 in S1 and S2, and to 80 in S1+S2. Even there it is not significant (unadjusted p = 0.065, Holm p = 0.20).

Independent recomputation. A separate check recomputed all 15 comparisons with Welch t-tests and a hand-written Holm correction, without using our main analysis code. The estimates and standard errors match to rounding error. Holm p-values differ by at most 0.0009, and every significance decision is the same (output/tables/verify_welch_holm.csv).

Reproduction of the published means. Our S1+S2 sample reproduces the original paper's mean effort in all 18 arms to within 4 points, with an average gap of 1.1 points. Its arm sizes are within 4 workers of the paper's, 9,831 against 9,861 in total. In the main sample, every arm is within 16 points (output/tables/paper_means_comparison.csv). The largest main-sample gap is the gift arm: 1,586 here against the paper's 1,602, which S1+S2 closes to 1,602. The gift's effect is therefore somewhat smaller under the registered rules than in the published sample. It is non-significant both in our main sample and in S1+S2.

5.6 Exploratory arms

The plan's exploratory comparisons are unadjusted and test no hypothesis (output/tables/exploratory.csv). Pay to the Red Cross raised effort less than the same pay to the worker: by 122 fewer points at 1 cent and 258 fewer at 10 cents. A 1% chance of $1 per 100 points raised it less than a sure 1 cent per 100, by 135 points. Three changes were not shown to differ from their comparison arms: delaying pay by two or four weeks, framing a threshold bonus as a loss rather than a gain, and a 50% chance of 2 cents.

6. What managers can take from this

Costless motivators work, but only part of the way. Each of the three raised effort by 14% to 22% over no pay:

  • asking workers to try as hard as they can, because the researchers are interested in the result;
  • promising to show them their rank;
  • telling them many others had scored above 2,000 points.

None matched a piece rate of 1 cent per 100 points, which raised effort by 33%. The request and the ranking also fell short of a piece rate so small it paid 1.5 cents on average. A manager who can pay even a trivial amount per unit of output should not expect requests for effort or rankings to substitute for it. These results do not test telling workers how their work helps others, which this experiment did not do.

Social comparison is the strongest of the three. A single sentence saying many people had scored above 2,000 points got about two-thirds of the effect of 1 cent per 100 points. It was not shown to differ from the token piece rate.

A small unconditional gift did not buy effort here. The 40-cent gift cost more per worker than either 1-cent piece rate paid on average: 1.5 cents at 1 cent per 1,000 points, and 20 cents at 1 cent per 100 (output/tables/exploratory_cost.csv). Yet it produced the smallest gain, and a non-significant one. This is one online test of a one-shot gift between strangers. It says little about gifts within ongoing employment relationships, where reciprocity has time and reason to operate.

Paying a little does not backfire. The worry that token pay signals the work is worthless and crowds out effort did not materialize: the smallest piece rate raised effort substantially. It got about three-quarters of the effect of 1 cent per 100 points. Going from 4 to 10 cents per 100 points added little that we could detect.

7. Limits

  • The setting is narrow. The task is ten minutes of key-pressing by online workers with no ongoing relationship with the employer. Effects of meaning, comparison and reciprocity in real jobs may be larger or smaller.
  • Each motivator is a sentence or two, much weaker than a sustained organizational practice.
  • The "task significance" arm is a request for effort. It does not show workers how their work helps others (section 2).
  • The gift test is weak for the reasons above.
  • Our plan was written after we had seen some published results. The agreement with the original paper confirms the published results but is not an independent replication.
  • The glitch window in S2 is our inference from the data, not a documented period, though it holds 719 starters against the 721 the original paper reports (output/tables/glitch_window.csv).

We draw on one further study only cautiously. Grant (2008) reports three field experiments on whether task significance increases job performance. We have verified only the opening of its abstract. Its subtitle refers to "boundary conditions," which may limit the effect to some workers. We therefore do not rely on it as establishing that task significance raises performance. Our "task significance" arm shows workers no beneficiary of their work, so our results do not bear on that claim either way.
