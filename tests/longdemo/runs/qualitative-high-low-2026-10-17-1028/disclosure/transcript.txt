I've written the full paper in `paper.md`. It runs about 4,900 words of prose plus a table, two figures and references, which comes to roughly 10 single-spaced pages. I couldn't render a PDF to confirm the count because the sandbox blocks the PDF tools. Every quotation (52 in the paper, 88 coded excerpts) matches the NASA transcripts word for word at the cited page; a script I wrote checks this, and I compared several page numbers against the PDFs by hand.

**The argument.** Three aggregate dimensions came out of the data:
- **Bounding:** before each accident, concerns sat with whichever part of the vehicle owned them, recurring problems were classed as "known" or as maintenance, and whoever raised a concern had to prove it.
- **Reopening:** each accident put everything back in question. Claims of safety now had to be demonstrated, and people were licensed to challenge.
- **Re-closing:** the flood of concerns was settled again by managers' judgment and by practitioners pruning precautions, while schedule pressure and loss of experience came back.

The main claim is that what an accident reopens follows how the failure is blamed. Challenger was blamed on the solid rocket motor, so the reopening stayed inside the motor, and debris hitting the orbiter stayed "known" until Columbia.

**Sample.** I read all ten candidates and kept eight: four engineers or technicians and four managers, covering both accidents. I dropped Odom, who says little about raising concerns. I dropped Moser because his transcript is from a different NASA oral history project and is mostly about Apollo and early design.

**Where I departed from your notes.** These items in `notes.md` were wrong, so the paper doesn't follow them:
1. **Vaughan (1996)** argued the opposite of amoral calculation. Her case is normalization of deviance: managers followed the rules and still made a mistake. The paper says this, and the data support it.
2. **Edmondson's** psychological safety paper is 1999, not 2001. ASQ 44(2), 350–383 is the 1999 paper.
3. **Columbia** was hit by foam from the External Tank's bipod ramp, not ice. Pessin even explains that the tank's insulation requirement was written to stop ice, not foam.
4. **The draft methods paragraph** says "we conducted ten interviews." We didn't: NASA historians did, and this is a secondary analysis of eight archival transcripts. I rewrote it.
5. **The Gioia method** works the other way round from your notes. It starts from informants' own terms and builds up to the aggregate dimensions; starting from literature categories is a deductive template. I did it inductively and compared the result with voice, normalization and learning afterward.
6. **"Verbatim" transcripts:** every PDF is headed "Edited Oral History Transcript," and some bracketed editorial additions run a full paragraph. The paper treats them as edited accounts.
7. **Roles in the candidate table:** Mitchell was the solid rocket motor project manager, not External Tank; Smith was the booster project manager rather than a redesign engineer; Sheehan was Columbia's vehicle manager before its first flight in 1981, not in 2003.
