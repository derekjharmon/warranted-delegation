Challenger and redesign side (Mitchell, Thomas, Smith):
1. "Testing was the ultimate referee": Mitchell p.15, "it was always test[ing] that was the ultimate referee for choosing the evolution of this redesign." Thomas p.11, "Everything redesigned and everything that was questioned prior to design had a test fixture or test stand to prove or disprove the hypothesis, or to demonstrate that the design was successful."
2. "Fresh eyeballs": Mitchell p.9, "have fresh eyeballs looking at different aspects of the redesign." Mitchell p.14, "that's healthy to have challenges to how design is proceeding."
3. "An independent voice that could holler 'Stop'": Mitchell p.13, "They wanted to make sure that there was an independent voice that could holler "Stop" and make sure safety was being adhered to."
4. "A little cleanliness": leaders brought in from outside the accident project. Mitchell p.5, "importantly was not on the project at the time of the accident."
5. "Look those people in the eye": being on site. Mitchell p.6, "to look those people in the eye, to turn around requests, to turn around studies, to turn around approvals, and do it quickly and on site."
6. "I had the final vote": Thomas p.9, "There was not necessarily always consensus, but we moved on as I had the final vote." Thomas p.14, "who did not have to get permission from many levels of management and organizations to implement designs, conduct tests, and perform analyses."
7. "You mean what?": Thomas p.12, "When intentionally flawing the motor first came up, I was among those that said, "You mean what?" But the more we thought about it, and the more confidence that we gained in our design, we thought it to be a good idea."
8. "It was changed": fixing what had no problem on paper. Mitchell p.16, "Even though this igniter had never had any problem with performance or otherwise, because on paper we didn't meet the 1.4 at the nozzle of that little motor, it was changed."
9. "Overzealous": Thomas p.20, "At one point the processing team overzealous with the number of measurements and inspections, like grease discoloration presence of human hair and lint, etc. It got to the point where we were not making much progress."
10. "A very personal beating": the cost of dissent. Mitchell p.10, "Roger [M.] Boisjoly—whom many people may have heard of, who opposed the launch and took a very personal beating emotionally and otherwise about the Challenger accident—thought the Vetco Gray idea was probably a very good one. He was pretty dismissive of our redesign that we eventually settled on, but it was all carefully considered."
11. "At their risk": the contractor acting before approval. Mitchell p.9, "At their risk—they didn't have NASA approval—Thiokol told [the machinists], "Do not finish conforming these cases.""

Foam, tile and Columbia side (Smith, Sheehan, Pessin, White, Singer, Moser):
12. "We do not do anything to this tile": Smith p.2, "I learned very quickly that we do not do anything to this tile." Sheehan p.11, "they [had] a "don't touch me" kind of attitude."
13. "I don't have a requirement": Sheehan p.11, "Almost as bad as the Tank guys were, "Well, I don't have a requirement to preclude ice." So everybody was very, very parochial." Sheehan p.7, "They wanted to be directed that that is a Program requirement."
14. "Eliminate ice, not foam": Pessin p.16, "We did not necessarily eliminate foam from coming off but needed to eliminate ice. That was the big driver."
15. "Asking hard questions": Pessin p.14, "some of them were rather pleased to leave after we got through asking hard questions. This was, to me, a valuable tool to bring the contractors in in an open forum."
16. "Bucket chemistry": Pessin p.18, "These subsuppliers have no idea where their product is going to be used. They have no idea what the effects of a change will be."
17. "It took a while to convince the program": White p.8, "I had a couple of people tell me in their earlier history that they were doing data analysis and came up with that issue, but it took a while to convince the program that it really was a problem."
18. "The technician gets a lot of help": White p.10, "that's a case where the technician gets a lot of help from a lot of engineering, but bottom line is he does it every day and his method of doing it came up with the most acceptable."
19. "Go check the other vehicles": White p.15, "When we found something out, we'd go check the other vehicles, and we do it not only with the tile; we do it with anything else."
20. "Our criteria changed": White p.18, "after the Columbia incident, our criteria changed." White p.19, "After the Columbia incident, they want us to take out any of the larger repairs, replace the tile, because we were concerned about one of those repairs coming out and becoming a high-speed bullet." White p.20, "They came here to KSC to brief the rest of the workforce about why you're having to take those and no longer something you've been flying for twenty years and now take it out, and here's why."
21. "I believe you, but demonstrate it": Singer p.15, "Before there may have been more of an acceptance that an element had done their homework and it was safe to fly. After Columbia, there was more effort to say, "I believe you, but demonstrate it.""
22. "Ask 'Why?'": Singer p.13, "The Columbia RTF effort gave the opportunity for all of us to question, not just accept a requirement or a way of doing business because that's the way you've always done it." Singer p.13, "It really challenged us to ask, "Why?""
23. "Did we make it safe enough?": Singer p.9, "we continuously asked ourselves, "Did we make it safe enough?"" Singer p.11, "with the heightened awareness of Columbia and the opportunity to relook, we said, "Let's go back and redesign the bolt catcher and make it safer.""
24. "Technically capable of challenging the contractor": Smith p.9, "We built a competency on the NASA side, where we were technically capable of challenging the contractor." Smith p.17, "we never flew something that we had not tested at least twice the duration on the ground."
25. "A gutsy call": Smith p.8, "It was a gutsy test, and I thought J.R. was absolutely out of his mind."
26. "The pressure of the schedule": Smith p.14, "we had pressure from our own management that kept looking at failure after failure in this marching schedule to get to flight, and I think that was felt by everybody on the project."
27. "I'd flunked the internal review": Smith p.19, "I'd flunked the internal review and I had to change the charts, in fact in ways I didn't even like."
28. "Bold and aggressive": Moser p.18, "We said we're going to be bold and aggressive on this thing, so we were." Moser pp.18–19, "We're going to take the risk. We think we understand what this is." Moser p.12, "You could not do that kind of redesign without having so many checks and balances in the system."
