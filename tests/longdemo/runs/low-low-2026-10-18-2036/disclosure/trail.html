Team Size, International Reach, and Citations in Management Research

Introduction

Scientific knowledge is now produced mostly by teams. Analysing decades of papers and patents, Wuchty et al. (2007) showed that teams increasingly dominate solo authors across nearly all fields, including the social sciences, and that team-authored papers are more frequently cited than solo-authored ones, an advantage that has grown over time. Larivière et al. (2015) traced the relationship between collaboration and citations back to 1900 and found that more authors, more institutional addresses, and more countries in a paper's byline are each associated with greater citation impact. Management research has followed the same path. In the leading management journals, single-authored articles have become the exception rather than the rule, and coauthors are often spread across institutions and countries.

If team papers are cited more, it is not obvious why. One possibility is size: a paper with more authors has more people to present it, more colleagues and students who hear about it, and more professional networks through which it can travel. A second possibility is reach: a team whose members work in different countries may carry a paper to scholarly communities that a team based in a single country would not reach, whatever its size. The two explanations are hard to separate because they move together, as larger teams are more likely to span countries. This paper asks whether international reach is associated with citations beyond what team size alone explains.

I examine 2,810 research articles published between 2008 and 2017 in four leading management journals, Administrative Science Quarterly, Academy of Management Journal, Strategic Management Journal, and Organization Science, using bibliographic and citation data from OpenAlex. I test two hypotheses:

  • H1: Papers with more authors receive more citations.
  • H2: Papers whose authors are based in more than one country receive more citations, beyond what team size alone explains.

I also explore an extension: whether it matters that a team includes at least one author whose institution is in a country outside the World Bank's high-income group.

The outcome is the number of citations each article received in its publication year and the five following calendar years, and the models are negative binomial regressions with journal and year fixed effects. The results support both hypotheses. Each additional author is associated with more citations, and international teams are cited more than single-country teams of the same size, although the international advantage is smaller when each author is assigned only to the country of their first-listed institution. I find no association between having an author from outside the high-income group and citations.

Theory and hypotheses

Team size

The most direct argument for a team-size advantage concerns diffusion. Each coauthor brings a personal network of colleagues, former students, collaborators, and conference audiences. A paper with more authors is presented in more seminars, cited in more of its authors' later work, and mentioned in more conversations, so more potential readers learn of it. Coauthors also bring different expertise, which may raise the quality of the work and widen the set of literatures to which it speaks.

The evidence across fields is consistent with this argument. Wuchty et al. (2007) found that team-authored papers receive more citations than solo-authored papers in the sciences and engineering, the social sciences, and the arts and humanities, and that teams increasingly produce the most highly cited work. Larivière et al. (2015) found that citation impact rises with the number of authors from the beginning of the twentieth century onward, and that this is not simply due to self-citation. They also found diminishing returns: as collaboration has become more common, larger teams are needed to achieve the same citation advantage. Whether the pattern holds within the narrow and highly selective set of papers published in the top management journals, where every paper has already cleared a demanding review process, is an open question.

H1: Papers with more authors receive more citations.

International reach

A second argument concerns the reach of a team rather than its size. Scholarly audiences are partly organized by country: through national associations and conferences, national doctoral programs, language, and the research questions that matter in particular economies. Coauthors based in different countries belong to different national communities and can carry a paper into each of them. Two coauthors in two countries may therefore reach more distinct readers than two coauthors at neighbouring universities, even though the teams are the same size. If this is right, the number of countries spanned by a team should be associated with citations even after the number of authors is held constant.

Larivière et al. (2015) found that citation impact rises with the number of countries in a paper's byline, as it does with the number of authors. Because international teams also tend to be larger, a country effect that ignores team size could simply be a size effect. The test of the reach argument is therefore whether an international team is cited more than a single-country team of the same size.

A related line of work concerns other forms of diversity within author teams. Freeman and Huang (2014), studying papers by U.S.-based authors, found that papers written by ethnically diverse teams received more citations than papers whose authors shared the same ethnicity. Their measure is ethnic diversity, which can occur entirely within one country, whereas the argument here concerns the geographic location of authors' institutions. The two are distinct: an ethnically diverse team may be based in a single country, and an international team may be ethnically homogeneous. The present study asks whether geographic spread carries a citation advantage of its own.

H2: Papers whose authors are based in more than one country receive more citations, beyond what team size alone explains.

Authors outside the high-income group

The reach argument suggests a further question. If international teams are cited more because they connect to different scholarly communities, the communities they connect to may matter. Management research is concentrated in high-income countries, and a coauthor based outside them may connect a paper to research communities that are growing but that are less often reached by work in these journals. At the same time, such communities may currently produce fewer citing papers in the journals indexed, and papers that involve them may address settings that the field's core audience reads less. The two considerations point in opposite directions, so I offer no directional hypothesis. I examine, as an exploratory extension, whether having at least one author whose institution is in a country outside the World Bank's high-income group is associated with citations, holding team size and international status constant.

Data and methods

Data source and sample

Bibliographic and citation data come from OpenAlex, an open index of scholarly works, authors, institutions, and citations, accessed through its public API in October 2026. I retrieved every work that OpenAlex lists under each journal's ISSN with a publication year from 2008 to 2017: Administrative Science Quarterly (ISSN 0001-8392), Academy of Management Journal (0001-4273), Strategic Management Journal (0143-2095), and Organization Science (1047-7039). The publication year recorded by OpenAlex is generally the year a paper first appeared online, which for journals that publish articles in advance can precede the year of the print issue. The ten-year window means that every paper in the sample has a complete five-year citation window.

The API returned 3,640 records. Journal records include many items that are not research articles, so I applied a sequence of filters, each recorded with its reason for every excluded record:

  1. Document type. I kept only records OpenAlex classifies as articles, which excluded front matter and other paratext (248 records), book reviews (67), editorials (41), errata (16), retraction notices (2), and four records typed as reviews.
  2. Retractions. Five articles flagged by OpenAlex as retracted were excluded.
  3. Missing authors. Fifty-seven records listed no authors.
  4. Titles. Seventy records had titles that identify non-research content, such as "Publications Received," "Notice to Contributors," "Errata," editorial notices, special-issue introductions, and anniversary essays.
  5. Journal DOIs. OpenAlex attaches some records from other sources to these journals. I kept records whose DOI belongs to the journal's publisher or to SSRN, since some genuine articles are indexed only under their SSRN DOI. This excluded 65 records with no DOI and 37 with a DOI from another source, most of them papers from a different journal with a similar name that OpenAlex had merged into the Organization Science record.
  6. Length. In Administrative Science Quarterly and Academy of Management Journal, items of 11 pages or fewer, or without pagination, are book reviews, "From the Editors" essays, or special-issue introductions rather than research articles; these rules excluded 85 short and 66 unpaginated records from ASQ and 43 short and 1 unpaginated record from AMJ. Strategic Management Journal and Organization Science publish short research notes and commentaries, so for these journals I excluded only items of 3 pages or fewer (11 in SMJ, 3 in Organization Science), which were errata repeating an original title or brief editors' notes, and 7 unpaginated SMJ items with no references, which were introductions to virtual special issues.
  7. Duplicates. Where the same title appeared twice in one journal, I kept one record, preferring the record with the journal's DOI; this removed 2 records. No two remaining records shared a DOI.

In total, 830 records were excluded and 2,810 research articles were kept: 208 from ASQ, 757 from AMJ, 1,042 from SMJ, and 803 from Organization Science. Commentaries and replies longer than the length thresholds remain in the sample.

Citations

The dependent variable is the number of citations a paper received in its first five years, defined as the publication year plus the five following calendar years. A paper published in 2010, for example, is credited with citations from works published in 2010 through 2015. Citations come from OpenAlex's year-by-year citation counts for each paper (its counts_by_year field), which record citations by the publication year of the citing work. These counts do not extend back to the earliest years of the window for the older papers in the sample. For those years I retrieved, for each paper, the works in OpenAlex that cite it, grouped by their publication year, and filled the missing years from that query.

Team size and country variables

Team size is the number of authors listed on the paper. The number of authors ranges from 1 to 17, but only two papers have more than eight authors, so in the regression models the author count is top-coded at 8; the descriptive statistics report the raw count.

Each author's country is taken from the institutions OpenAlex lists for that author on the paper, using the country code OpenAlex assigns to each institution. An author listed with institutions in more than one country is counted in each of those countries. Authors with no listed institution, or whose institutions have no country code, are kept in the author count but contribute no country; across the sample, 91 of 7,102 authorships have no country, affecting 84 papers. The number of distinct countries on a paper is the number of distinct country codes among its authors' institutions, and a paper is coded as an international team if its authors span two or more countries. Six papers have no author with any country; for these papers the country variables are missing, and they are excluded from the models that include country variables but kept in the team-size model. The country sample therefore contains 2,804 papers, of which 1,220 are international and 1,584 are single-country.

Income classification

For the extension, each country is assigned to an income group using the World Bank's income classification for fiscal year 2017, the classification in force at the end of the sample window, applied to every paper regardless of its publication year. The World Bank's historical classification file could not be downloaded in this project's computing environment, so the FY2017 classification was rebuilt from the World Bank's own published inputs, retrieved through its public API: each country's 2015 gross national income (GNI) per capita (Atlas method) from the July 2016 release of the World Development Indicators (the release on which the FY2017 classification was based), compared against the FY2017 income thresholds. OpenAlex's two-letter country codes were converted to the World Bank's three-letter codes using the World Bank's country list.

Five countries could not be classified this way and were classified by hand, each with its reason recorded in the country table. Taiwan does not appear in the World Bank's data and is classified as high income, as it is generally treated. Argentina has no 2015 GNI value in the July 2016 release; its value in a later 2016 release (12,460) is below the high-income threshold of 12,475, and it is classified as upper middle income. Macao is classified as high income on the basis of its GNI values in releases from later in 2016. Iran and Cuba have no 2015 values and are classified as upper middle income, Iran on the basis of its most recent available GNI and Cuba on the basis of the World Bank's classification of it as upper middle income.

The 54 countries in the sample comprise 35 high-income, 14 upper-middle-income, and 5 lower-middle-income countries; none is low income. The variable any non-high-income author equals 1 if at least one author's institution is in a country outside the high-income group and 0 otherwise. Authors without a country do not count, and the variable is missing for the six papers with no country.

Models

Because citations are overdispersed counts, I estimate negative binomial (NB2) regressions, with the dispersion parameter estimated by maximum likelihood. All models include journal and year fixed effects and use heteroskedasticity-robust (HC1) standard errors. Model 1 includes the author count only and uses all 2,810 papers (H1). Model 2 includes the international-team indicator only. Model 3 includes both (H2). Model 4 adds the any-non-high-income indicator (the extension). Models 2 through 4 use the 2,804 papers with country data. Coefficients are reported as incidence rate ratios (IRRs), the multiplicative change in expected citations for a one-unit change in the predictor, with delta-method standard errors.

Robustness check

Counting an author in every country where they hold an affiliation means that a single author with affiliations in two countries can make a paper international. As a robustness check, I rebuild the country variables using only each author's first-listed institution and re-estimate Models 3 and 4. Under this coding, 117 papers that are international under the main coding become single-country, and 16 papers that have a non-high-income author under the main coding no longer do; no paper loses all of its countries, so the sample remains 2,804 papers.

International teams and citations

On its own, international status is associated with more citations: in Model 2 the incidence rate ratio is 1.158 (SE 0.041, p < 0.001). When the number of authors is added in Model 3, the ratio falls to 1.114 (SE 0.041, p < 0.01). Part of the raw international advantage reflects the fact that international teams tend to be larger, but international teams are still cited more than single-country teams of the same size. This supports H2.

Figure 2 shows how the comparison varies with team size. Among two-author papers, international and single-country papers are cited about equally: single-country papers (n = 647) average 66.38 citations and international papers (n = 446) 63.84. Among three-author papers, international papers (n = 511) average 68.57 and single-country papers (n = 413) 62.10. The gap is largest among papers with four or more authors, where international papers (n = 244) average 86.68 citations (95% CI 76.50 to 96.85) and single-country papers (n = 152) average 69.48 (95% CI 59.49 to 79.47). The international advantage estimated in Model 3 is thus concentrated among larger teams. Model 3 imposes a single international effect across team sizes.

Authors outside the high-income group

Model 4 adds the indicator for any author based in a country outside the high-income group. Its incidence rate ratio is 0.962 (SE 0.057), which is not statistically significant, and adding it barely changes the log-likelihood (from −14,198.8 to −14,198.6). The coefficients on team size (1.065) and international status (1.119) are essentially unchanged from Model 3. There is no evidence in these data that having an author from outside the high-income group is associated with citations, in either direction, once team size and international status are taken into account.

Discussion

This study asked whether articles in four leading management journals written by larger teams, and by teams spread across countries, receive more citations, and whether any international advantage survives once team size is held constant.

The answer to the first question is yes. Papers with more authors receive more citations in their first five years, and the association holds with journal and year held constant and with international status controlled. The descriptive pattern is less smooth than a single coefficient implies. The clearest steps are from one author to two, and from three authors to four; two- and three-author papers are cited at almost the same rate; and the few papers with five or more authors are too rare in these journals to say much about. H1 is supported, but the model's assumption that each additional author adds the same proportional increase is a simplification of that pattern.

The answer to the second question is also yes, with a qualification. International teams are cited more than single-country teams, and most of the raw international advantage remains when team size is controlled: the incidence rate ratio falls from 1.158 to 1.114. This is consistent with the reach argument: coauthors in different countries appear to bring a paper to audiences that a same-sized team in a single country does not. The qualification is that the advantage depends on how an author's country is defined. When each author is assigned only to the country of their first-listed institution, the international advantage is smaller (1.086) and less precisely estimated, though still statistically significant. Some of the advantage in the main models comes from authors who are themselves affiliated in two countries rather than from coauthors located in different countries. Figure 2 adds a second qualification: the international advantage is concentrated among teams of three or more authors and absent among two-author teams. Reach may matter most when it is combined with size, which is a question the present models, with a single international coefficient, do not address. H2 is supported, but the size of the effect should be read with both qualifications in mind.

These findings sit alongside Freeman and Huang's (2014) evidence that ethnically diverse author teams are cited more than ethnically homogeneous ones. Both results point to the value of teams that combine members from different backgrounds or communities, but they concern different dimensions of diversity. The present results show that geographic spread across countries carries a citation advantage in management research beyond team size; they do not speak to the ethnic composition of teams.

The exploratory extension found nothing. Papers with at least one author based in a country outside the high-income group are cited neither more nor less than other papers with the same team size and international status. Such papers are uncommon in these journals (a proportion of 0.057 of the country sample), and almost all of them are also international (151 of 160), so the comparison rests mainly on how these papers differ from other international papers. Within these limits, there is no sign that the income level of coauthors' countries is associated with how much a paper is cited.

Taken together, the results suggest that both who is on a team and where they are located matter for how widely management research is cited. Larger teams are cited more, and teams that span countries are cited more again, while the income level of the countries involved is not associated with citations.

This paper contributes to research on team science in management by separating two features that usually travel together, team size and international reach, in four leading management journals over a decade. Larger teams are cited more, and international teams are cited more again at a given team size, which suggests that reach adds something that headcount alone does not. The study has limits. It is observational, so the estimates are associations and not effects of team composition on citations; stronger papers may attract larger and more international teams. Affiliation country is a rough stand-in for the audiences a coauthor brings, and OpenAlex's author and institution data contain errors. Four journals are a narrow slice of management research, and the non-high-income comparison rests on 160 papers, only nine of them single-country. Finally, citations are an imperfect measure of influence.
