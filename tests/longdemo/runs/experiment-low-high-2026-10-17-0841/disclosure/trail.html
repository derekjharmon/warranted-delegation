What Motivates Effort on Dull Work? A Preregistered Reanalysis of DellaVigna and Pope (2018)

Abstract

We reanalyze an online experiment by DellaVigna and Pope (2018) in which Mechanical Turk workers were randomly assigned to one of 18 ways of motivating a dull, 10-minute key-pressing task. Our sample has 9,963 workers, against the authors' 9,861, because one of their exclusions cannot be reproduced from the raw data. Every piece rate raised effort over no piece rate, including 1 cent per 1,000 points. Paying 4 cents per 100 points raised effort over 1 cent, but 10 cents could not be distinguished from 4 cents. Social comparison, ranking and a task-significance message each raised effort over no piece rate, but all three fell short of 1 cent per 100 points. Ranking and task significance also fell short of the smallest piece rate. For social comparison, that comparison is inconclusive. An unconditional 40-cent gift did not raise effort significantly. Our tests follow a preregistered plan, but the plan was written after the published results were public.

1. Introduction

Organizations motivate people with more than pay: reminders of why the work matters, comparisons with coworkers, rankings, and unexpected gifts meant to build goodwill. Managers choosing among these tools need to know how much effort each buys, compared with simply paying a little more per unit of work. As DellaVigna and Pope (2018, p. 1) note, "Different dependent variables and dissimilar participant samples can make direct comparisons of effect sizes across various studies difficult."

DellaVigna and Pope (2018) ran a large online experiment that compares 18 ways of motivating the same dull task within a single design. We reanalyze the raw data from the authors' replication package for an organizational audience, asking which motivators raise effort without raising pay, and how they compare with paying by the piece. Before analyzing outcomes in our data we wrote and froze an analysis plan, which states three hypotheses:

  • H1. Paying a piece rate raises effort over paying nothing per unit, and a higher piece rate raises it more.
  • H2. Psychological motivators with no extra pay (social comparison, ranking and task significance) raise effort over no piece rate, but less than even the smallest piece rate.
  • H3. An unconditional gift raises effort through reciprocity. This is the plan's hypothesis, stated without a supporting citation.

Two of the arms draw on earlier work, as DellaVigna and Pope describe it. They design their very-low-pay arm "as in Gneezy and Rustichini (2000)" (DellaVigna and Pope, 2018, p. 8). Their task-significance arm "is based on the influential literature in psychology on task significance (Grant, 2008)" (DellaVigna and Pope, 2018, p. 11). We cite these two works only for how DellaVigna and Pope used them. We did not check the original articles, and we make no claim about what either one found or how it defines its terms.

Section 7 (Contribution) sets out what this reanalysis adds.

2. Data and Design

The experiment. DellaVigna and Pope (2018) recruited workers on Amazon Mechanical Turk for a $1 flat fee. In the raw file, start dates run from 15 May to 5 June 2015; the authors describe the experiment as running "for three weeks in May 2015" (p. 14). After three demographic questions, workers had 10 minutes to press the "a" and "b" keys alternately as fast as they could, earning one point for each a-b alternation. Each worker was randomly assigned to one of 18 treatment arms, which differed only in one paragraph of the task instructions. The study was preregistered on the AEA RCT Registry (AEARCTR-0000714).1

Table A1 (Appendix) lists all 18 arms. The arms our hypotheses use are worded as follows:

  • Gift exchange: "In appreciation to you for performing this task, you will be paid a bonus of 40 cents. Your score will not affect your payment in any way." (DellaVigna and Pope, 2018, p. 8).
  • Social comparison: "Your score will not affect your payment in any way. In a previous version of this task, many participants were able to score more than 2,000 points." (DellaVigna and Pope, 2018, p. 52).
  • Ranking: "Your score will not affect your payment in any way. After you play, we will show you how well you did relative to other participants who have previously done this task." (DellaVigna and Pope, 2018, p. 52).
  • Task significance: "Your score will not affect your payment in any way. We are interested in how fast people choose to press digits and we would like you to do your very best. So please try as hard as you can." (DellaVigna and Pope, 2018, p. 11).

DellaVigna and Pope (2018, p. 11) group the last three under the heading "Psychology-based Treatments". They describe ranking as a "second social-comparison treatment" (p. 11), so H2 covers two kinds of motivator: social comparison (two arms) and task significance (one arm). Arm 9's wording asks for effort but does not mention anyone who benefits from the work. DellaVigna and Pope base the arm on Grant (2008), but we did not check the original article. We therefore make no claim about how closely the arm matches Grant (2008).

Data. We use the raw data file from the authors' replication package, as it was before their cleaning and without per-press timestamps. It has one row for each of the 12,838 workers who started the task, the same initial sample the authors report (DellaVigna and Pope, 2018, p. 14). It records points scored, start and end times, completion, bonus paid, practice-round presses, treatment code and approval status.

Arm labels. The file identifies arms by numeric code. We labeled the codes from a code table in our project notes, which we could not check against the authors' files. Payouts confirm each code's pay scheme. But four groups of codes pay identically and cannot be told apart from the data: {1.1, 3.1, 4.1, 4.2}, {1.2, 3.2}, {5.1, 5.2} and {1.3, 7, 8, 9}. Within those groups the labels rest on the code table.

Sample. Table 1 shows how the sample was built, in the order the authors apply their exclusions (DellaVigna and Pope, 2018, pp. 14–15).

We apply the three exclusions in the registration, as quoted by DellaVigna and Pope (2018, p. 14), then the two restrictions the authors added (p. 15), and finally drop rows with no treatment code, which cannot be assigned to an arm. The registration excludes scores of 4,000 points or more as likely bots, because a pilot showed that more than 3,500 points is "physically impossible". The notes to Table 1 give our operational definitions.

The authors also dropped 721 workers affected by a malfunction of the score counter when the survey software moved to a new server (p. 14). The raw file does not flag these workers, and the paper gives no time window, so we do not reconstruct this exclusion.

Our final sample has 9,963 workers, 102 (1.0%) more than the authors' 9,861. The difference is spread evenly across arms: each has 0 to 9 more workers than the authors report (532–577 per arm, against their "about 550"; p. 15). Section 6 compares our arm means with the published ones.

3. Methods

Preregistration. We wrote an analysis plan and attested it on 17 October 2026, before computing any outcome statistic from the data. Until then we had examined only sample counts, missing values, payout mechanics (to check the treatment codes) and the cleaning steps. Any deviation from the plan is reported in Section 5, "Deviations from the plan".

What was already known. This is a reanalysis of a published experiment, so its findings were public before the plan was written. The AI assistant used in this project (Claude) had seen DellaVigna and Pope's (2018) arm means and summary findings before it helped draft the plan. The author chose the hypotheses, comparisons and robustness samples; after seeing those findings, the assistant proposed treating the comparisons with the 1-cent piece rate as a separate multiple-comparison family.

Outcome and model. The outcome is points scored. We estimate one OLS regression of points on indicators for all 18 arms, with no piece rate (arm 1.3) as the reference and no covariates, and use HC1 heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors. Each estimate is a difference in mean points between two arms:

  • a comparison with the no-piece-rate arm is the t-test on that arm's coefficient;
  • a comparison between two other arms is a test that their two coefficients are equal, within the same regression.

All tests are two-sided at α = 0.05.

Tests. H1 compares each piece rate, including 1 cent per 1,000 points, with no piece rate and tests two orderings (10 against 4 cents, 4 against 1 cent); H2 compares each psychological arm with no piece rate and with the smallest piece rate (primary family) and with 1 cent per 100 points (secondary family); H3 compares gift exchange with no piece rate. Table 2 lists all 16 tests by family, and the remaining arms are reported descriptively only (Table A1, Appendix).

Multiple comparisons. We apply the Holm correction within each family (H1, H2 primary, H2 secondary, H3) and report both unadjusted and adjusted p-values.

Robustness. We re-estimate the same tests on two other samples:

  • Complete cases: workers with no missing points, treatment or practice-round count (N = 8,053). Practice counts are missing for 1,910 workers, so this checks whether results depend on those workers; it is not a cleaner sample.
  • Trimmed: the primary sample with scores above 3,500 removed, the registration's threshold for scores impossible by hand (N = 9,950).

These results are reported alongside the primary ones, without separate inference claims.

Comparison with the published estimates. Outside the plan, we compare our arm means with those reported by DellaVigna and Pope (2018, Table 4) for the nine arms in our hypotheses. This is a descriptive check only, with no tests.

4.1 H1: Piece rates

Every piece rate raised effort over no piece rate, including 1 cent per 1,000 points (Table 2; all four p < 0.001, unadjusted and Holm-adjusted).

On the ordering, 4 cents raised effort over 1 cent by 99 points (24 to 173; p = 0.009, Holm p = 0.019). The difference between 10 cents and 4 cents was not significant: 43 points (−27 to 113; p = 0.226, Holm p = 0.226).

H1 is supported for paying something over paying nothing. Its claim that a higher piece rate raises effort more is supported from 1 cent to 4 cents but not from 4 cents to 10 cents.

4.2 H2: Psychological motivators

Each psychological arm raised effort over no piece rate (Table 2; all three p < 0.001, unadjusted and Holm-adjusted). Against the smallest piece rate (arm 2, 1 cent per 1,000 points), ranking and task significance raised effort less (Holm p = 0.004 and Holm p < 0.001).

Social comparison raised points relative to no piece rate by 334 (95% CI 247 to 421). Its difference from the smallest piece rate was −30 points (95% CI −114 to +54; Holm-adjusted p = 0.48). The data therefore neither show that social comparison is less effective than the smallest piece rate nor that it is as effective. They are consistent with a shortfall of up to about 114 points, or an advantage of up to about 54. For arm 7, the "less than even the smallest piece rate" part of H2 is inconclusive. It is supported for ranking (arm 8) and task significance (arm 9).

In the secondary family, all three psychological arms fell short of 1 cent per 100 points (Table 2; all p < 0.001, unadjusted and Holm-adjusted).

4.3 H3: Gift exchange

The unconditional 40-cent gift raised points over no piece rate by 72 (95% CI −13 to 156; p = 0.095). With a single test in the family, the Holm p-value is the same. The difference is not significant. The interval includes zero, and also includes gains of up to about 156 points.

4.4 Robustness

Table A2 (Appendix) repeats the H2 and H3 tests in the complete-case sample (N = 8,053) and in the sample trimmed at 3,500 points (N = 9,950).

  • Trimmed sample: every conclusion above is unchanged.
  • Complete-case sample, ranking vs the smallest piece rate: the estimate is −98 points (−189 to −7; p = 0.034). After the Holm correction it is not significant (Holm p = 0.068).
  • Complete-case sample, gift exchange: 60 points (−32 to 152; p = 0.200), again not significant.
  • Complete-case sample, everything else: the other H2 conclusions are unchanged.
  • H1, both samples: the conclusions are also unchanged (not tabulated). 10 cents vs 4 cents remains not significant, with Holm p = 0.109 and 0.185. 4 cents vs 1 cent remains significant, with Holm p = 0.010 and 0.031.

5. Deviations from the plan

There were no deviations in the sample rules, outcome, model, tests, multiple-comparison families or robustness samples. For completeness:

  • Additions not in the plan. Two outputs were added:

    • Figure 1 and the per-arm confidence intervals behind it, which are descriptive;
    • the comparison with the published estimates in Section 6.

    Neither involves a hypothesis test.

  • Implementation. The plan describes comparisons between two non-reference arms as Wald tests. We computed each as a t-test on the difference between the two coefficients. With a single restriction this gives the same p-value, and it also yields a signed estimate and a confidence interval.

  • Changes after attestation.

    • A disclosure section was appended to the plan, below its frozen text, stating which published results were known before it was written (Section 3).
    • The cleaning script was changed to also save its step counts as a table. The cleaned data are byte-identical before and after the change.

  • Wording dropped from the plan. The plan says arm 9's wording tests task significance in Grant's (2008) sense "only loosely". The paper drops that statement because we could not check Grant's definition (Section 2).

6. Comparison with the published estimates (unplanned)

As a descriptive check outside the plan, we compared our arm means with those reported in DellaVigna and Pope (2018, Table 4) for the nine arms in our hypotheses.

  • Arm means: ours are within 11 points of theirs.
  • Gaps from no piece rate: ours are within 10 points of theirs. The largest differences are for gift exchange (−9) and social comparison (+7), both small next to standard errors of about 43 points.

The extra 102 workers in our sample therefore do not appear to change comparisons materially in these nine arms. Arm 7's mean matches the published social-comparison mean. The check has two limits:

  • it cannot tell ranking from task significance, whose published means differ by only 21 points;
  • it does not cover the charity, delayed-pay and threshold codes.

7. Discussion

These results come from one dull, 10-minute key-pressing task done by Mechanical Turk workers for a $1 fee. They show how the motivators compare in that setting. They cannot tell us whether the same pattern holds for other tasks, longer jobs or real workplaces.

Pay (H1). Every piece rate raised effort over paying nothing per unit, including 1 cent per 1,000 points. Raising the rate from 1 to 4 cents per 100 points added effort, but we could not distinguish 10 cents from 4 cents. H1 is supported for paying something over paying nothing; its ordering is supported from 1 cent to 4 cents but not from 4 cents to 10 cents.

Motivators without extra pay (H2). Social comparison, ranking and the task-significance message each raised effort over no piece rate, so in this setting some extra effort came without extra pay per unit. All three fell short of 1 cent per 100 points. Ranking and task significance also fell short of the smallest piece rate, though in one robustness sample the ranking shortfall was not significant after correction. For social comparison, the comparison with the smallest piece rate is inconclusive: the data neither show that social comparison is less effective than a token piece rate, nor that it is as effective.

Gift (H3). The unconditional 40-cent gift did not raise effort significantly (95% CI −13 to 156 points). Its confidence interval includes zero as well as modest gains, so the data neither show that the gift worked nor rule out a small effect.

Contribution

This paper adds a managerial reading to a data set that was built for economists. In one design, with one task and one pool of workers, it puts the common non-pay motivators (social comparison, ranking, a plain appeal to effort) next to piece rates of several sizes and to an unconditional gift. For an organizational audience, the useful result is a ranking within that design. Each of the three no-pay messages raised effort over paying nothing per unit, but all of them fell short of a 1-cent-per-100-points piece rate, and ranking and the task-significance message also fell short of a piece rate of 1 cent per 1,000 points. The unconditional gift did not raise effort by a margin we can distinguish from zero. A manager weighing a message against a small payment can therefore see how much effort the message bought here, and where it stopped. I do not claim that the same ordering holds for other tasks, longer jobs, or workers who stay with an employer, nor that the task-significance arm tests the construct as the organizational literature defines it. I did not check that literature against the original articles for this paper.

Limitations

First, this is one task: pressing two keys for ten minutes, for a $1 fee, done by online workers who had no relationship with the requester. Motivation in a workplace with colleagues, a manager and a future with the employer may work very differently, and so may the value of a message or a gift. Second, our sample is not the authors' sample. We end with 9,963 workers against their 9,861, because the raw file does not identify the 721 workers they dropped for a server malfunction, and some of our extra workers may have mismeasured scores. Our arm means are close to the published ones, but this does not show that the samples are the same. Third, the arm labels for four groups of codes rest on our notes, since payouts cannot separate them, and the check against the published means cannot tell ranking from task significance. Fourth, arm 9's wording does not mention who benefits from the work, and I did not read Grant (2008), so I cannot say how well it tests task significance. Fifth, the plan was written after the published results were public, and our assistant had seen some of them. The tests are preregistered in our sense only. Sixth, the analysis is confined to total points. It says nothing about work quality, later effort, or whether a message loses its effect when it is repeated. Seventh, the inconclusive result for social comparison against the smallest piece rate, and the non-significant gift effect, are not evidence of equivalence or of no effect.
